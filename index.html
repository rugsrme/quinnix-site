Defining Development

Software development is an evolving process. A tool that starts small doesn't have to stay that way.

An application may begin as a small widget designed to solve one immediate problem. As needs change, functionality expands, features mature, and the original utility becomes a complete application. That progression is the definition of development.

Arctic Link is a good example. It started as a simple SSH connection manager — a widget for opening remote terminals without remembering hostnames and options. Over time, it grew to include hardware monitoring, local AI model management, conversation history, and more. It didn't start that way. It became that through continued use, real problems, and incremental work.

Quinnix builds software this way: start with something useful, solve the real problem, and let the application grow into what it actually needs to be.

From BASIC to AI — and Everything in Between

Quinn Stewart

I'm Quinn Stewart, a lifelong computer enthusiast, Linux tinkerer, and independent software developer.

My journey into programming began around 1982, when I took a BASIC programming course offered through a community college at my high school. I was a freshman, earning college credit and discovering something that would fascinate me for decades.

Not long afterward, I got a Texas Instruments TI-99/4A for Christmas. Programs were saved on cassette tapes, graphics were built with hexadecimal values and binary patterns, and getting a computer to do something interesting meant figuring out how to make it happen yourself.

By my sophomore year, I was helping other students learn programming. Eventually, though, cars, friends, and life took priority, and computers took a back seat for a while.

I returned to computing in the mid-1990s, just as personal computers and the internet were taking off. Before long, I discovered Linux. I spent countless hours compiling kernels, configuring hardware, experimenting with distributions like Gentoo, and learning how the pieces fit together.

That curiosity eventually carried me into web development, working with technologies like PHP and JavaScript, and building applications to solve problems I encountered along the way.

Today, I'm exploring another remarkable shift in computing: artificial intelligence. I run local language models on my own Linux hardware, experiment with speech synthesis and voice generation, and use AI-assisted development tools to build applications that would once have required considerably more time and resources.

Projects like Arctic Link and VoxMason began with practical needs and grew into something much more capable. That's the idea behind Quinnix Software.

From Widgets to Working Applications.

After more than four decades of watching computers evolve, the part I enjoy most hasn't changed: learning something new, figuring out how it works, and seeing what I can build with it.
